LANDMARK SUPREME COURT JUDGMENTS FOR CIVIL CASES (SHORT HEADNOTES)
Source type: Starter notes. These are brief summaries for retrieval. Read the full judgment (Supreme Court website, Indian Kanoon, SCC Online) before citing.

### Indian Oil Corporation v. NEPC India Ltd., (2006) 6 SCC 736 | Civil disputes given criminal colour
Topic: misuse of criminal law (cheating, breach of trust) in commercial/civil disputes.
Held: The Supreme Court noted a growing tendency in business circles to convert purely civil disputes into criminal cases to pressure the other side into settlement. Criminal proceedings can be quashed where the complaint, even if taken at face value, discloses only a civil dispute. However, if the facts also disclose a criminal offence, the existence of a civil remedy does not bar criminal prosecution.
Relevance: defending a cheating/breach of trust complaint arising from a contract or money dispute; quashing under BNSS Section 528 (old CrPC 482).

### Hridaya Ranjan Prasad Verma v. State of Bihar, (2000) 4 SCC 168 | Breach of contract vs cheating
Held: The distinction between mere breach of contract and cheating depends on the intention of the accused at the time of the inducement. Fraudulent or dishonest intention must be shown to exist at the beginning of the transaction. Mere failure to keep a promise later does not make it cheating.
Relevance: IPC 420 / BNS 318(4) complaints arising out of failed sale agreements, unpaid advances, business deals.

### Mohd. Ibrahim v. State of Bihar, (2009) 8 SCC 751 | Selling property one does not own is not forgery
Held: Executing a sale deed in respect of land which the executant does not own, claiming it as his own, does not amount to "making a false document" and hence is not forgery under IPC 464/467/471. The aggrieved party's remedy, if any, is to approach the civil court. The Court also cautioned against criminal complaints in essentially civil property disputes.
Relevance: property title disputes, double sale; forgery provisions now in BNS 335, 336, 338, 340.

### Priyanka Srivastava v. State of U.P., (2015) 6 SCC 287 | Affidavit required for Magistrate-directed investigation
Held: Applications to a Magistrate seeking a direction to police to register and investigate (CrPC 156(3), now BNSS 175(3)) must be supported by an affidavit of the applicant, so that people do not file such applications casually to settle civil scores or pressurise others.
Relevance: criminal complaints filed as pressure tactics in civil/money disputes.

### Dalpat Kumar v. Prahlad Singh, (1992) 1 SCC 719 | Tests for temporary injunction
Held: To grant a temporary injunction under Order 39 CPC, the court must be satisfied about (1) a prima facie case, (2) irreparable injury that cannot be compensated in money if the injunction is refused, and (3) the balance of convenience in favour of the applicant. These are matters of judicial discretion.
Relevance: every stay/injunction application in property, partnership or contract suits.

### Rame Gowda v. M. Varadappa Naidu, (2004) 1 SCC 769 | Protection of settled possession
Held: A person in settled possession of property, even without title, can be dispossessed only by due process of law; even the true owner cannot forcibly dispossess him. Possession must be effective, undisturbed and to the knowledge of the owner for a sufficiently long period to be "settled".
Relevance: injunction suits by persons in possession; Specific Relief Act Section 6 suits after forcible dispossession.

### Anathula Sudhakar v. P. Buchi Reddy, (2008) 4 SCC 594 | Injunction simpliciter vs suit for declaration
Held: A suit for injunction simpliciter is concerned only with possession. Where the plaintiff's title is under a cloud or in dispute, and he is not in possession, he must sue for declaration of title and possession/consequential injunction; issues of title should not ordinarily be decided in a suit for mere injunction.
Relevance: choosing the right relief while drafting property suits (Specific Relief Act Sections 34 and 38).

### Suraj Lamp & Industries (P) Ltd. v. State of Haryana, (2012) 1 SCC 656 | GPA sales do not convey title
Held: Transfers of immovable property through Sale Agreement / General Power of Attorney / Will (SA-GPA-Will) are not "transfers" or "sales" and do not convey title. Immovable property can be legally and lawfully transferred only by a registered deed of conveyance (TPA Section 54, Registration Act Section 17). Genuine GPA transactions for other purposes are not affected.
Relevance: buying/selling property on GPA, title disputes, bank loans on GPA property.

### Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma, (2020) 9 SCC 1 | Daughters as coparceners
Held (three-judge bench): The amended Section 6 of the Hindu Succession Act confers coparcenary status on daughters by birth, with the same rights as sons. It is not necessary that the father (coparcener) was alive on 9 September 2005. A plea of oral partition cannot ordinarily defeat the daughter's claim and is accepted only in exceptional cases backed by public documents; partitions before 20 December 2004 by registered deed or court decree are protected.
Relevance: sisters' partition suits for ancestral property.

### Ravinder Kaur Grewal v. Manjit Kaur, (2019) 8 SCC 729 | Adverse possession as a sword
Held (three-judge bench): A person who has perfected title by adverse possession for the statutory period of 12 years can file a suit for declaration of title and for protection of possession; adverse possession can be used as a sword as well as a shield. Once the period is complete, the owner's title is extinguished (Limitation Act Section 27) and vests in the possessor.
Relevance: long-standing occupation disputes; Limitation Act Articles 64 and 65.

### Kailash v. Nanhku, (2005) 4 SCC 480 | Time to file written statement in ordinary suits
Held: The 90-day outer limit in Order 8 Rule 1 CPC for filing a written statement is directory, not mandatory, in ordinary civil suits. Courts may extend time in exceptional cases for reasons recorded in writing, and may impose costs.
Relevance: defendants who missed the deadline in a regular civil suit.

### SCG Contracts (India) Pvt. Ltd. v. K.S. Chamankar Infrastructure Pvt. Ltd., (2019) 12 SCC 210 | Commercial suits - 120 days is mandatory
Held: In commercial suits governed by the Commercial Courts Act, 2015, the written statement must be filed within 120 days of service of summons; after 120 days the defendant forfeits the right and the court cannot take the written statement on record.
Relevance: commercial disputes of specified value (Rs 3 lakh and above).

### Patil Automation Pvt. Ltd. v. Rakheja Engineers Pvt. Ltd., (2022) 10 SCC 1 | Pre-institution mediation mandatory in commercial suits
Held: Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 is mandatory. A commercial suit that does not contemplate urgent interim relief, filed without pre-institution mediation, is liable to be rejected under Order 7 Rule 11 CPC. The declaration was made effective from 20 August 2022.
Relevance: filing commercial recovery suits.

### T. Arivandandam v. T.V. Satyapal, (1977) 4 SCC 467 | Nip frivolous suits in the bud
Held: If on a meaningful reading of the plaint it is manifestly vexatious and without merit, and clever drafting has created only an illusion of a cause of action, the trial court should reject the plaint under Order 7 Rule 11 CPC at the threshold.
Relevance: defendant's application for rejection of plaint.

### S.P. Chengalvaraya Naidu v. Jagannath, (1994) 1 SCC 1 | Fraud vitiates everything
Held: A judgment or decree obtained by playing fraud on the court is a nullity and can be challenged in any court, even in collateral proceedings. A litigant who approaches the court must come with clean hands; one who withholds a vital document to gain advantage commits fraud on the court.
Relevance: challenging decrees obtained by suppression or forged documents.

### Dalip Singh v. State of U.P., (2010) 2 SCC 114 | Suppression of facts
Held: A litigant who does not come to court with clean hands and suppresses material facts is not entitled to be heard on merits, and the court can dismiss the case at the threshold.
Relevance: injunction applications and writ petitions where facts are concealed.

### Salem Advocate Bar Association v. Union of India, (2005) 6 SCC 344 | CPC amendments and ADR
Held: Upheld the validity of the 1999 and 2002 CPC amendments and approved model case management rules and mediation rules, giving effect to Section 89 CPC (referral to arbitration, conciliation, Lok Adalat, mediation, judicial settlement).
Relevance: court-referred mediation in civil suits.

### Meters and Instruments Pvt. Ltd. v. Kanchan Mehta, (2018) 1 SCC 560 | Cheque bounce cases are primarily compensatory
Held: The offence under Section 138 NI Act is primarily a civil wrong with a compensatory aim; courts should encourage compounding, and if the accused pays the cheque amount with reasonable interest and costs, the court can close the proceedings. Summary trial procedure should generally be followed.
Relevance: settling cheque bounce complaints; parallel recovery suits.

### Amardeep Singh v. Harveen Kaur, (2017) 8 SCC 746 | Waiver of 6-month cooling period
Held: The 6-month waiting period under Section 13B(2) Hindu Marriage Act for mutual consent divorce is directory; the court can waive it where the parties have been separated for over 18 months, mediation has failed, and all issues (alimony, custody) are settled.
Relevance: speedy mutual consent divorce.

### Rajnesh v. Neha, (2021) 2 SCC 324 | Maintenance guidelines
Held: Laid down guidelines on overlapping maintenance claims under different laws (HMA, DV Act, CrPC 125/BNSS 144), mandatory filing of an affidavit of disclosure of assets and liabilities by both parties, the date from which maintenance is payable (date of application), and criteria for determining the amount.
Relevance: maintenance claims in family courts.

### Satish Chander Ahuja v. Sneha Ahuja, (2021) 1 SCC 414 | Shared household
Held (three-judge bench): Under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, a "shared household" includes the household where the woman lived in a domestic relationship, even if it belongs to the husband's relatives (e.g. in-laws) and the husband has no right in it. The right of residence is not an indefeasible right of ownership and must be balanced with the rights of the owners.
Relevance: in-laws' eviction suits against daughter-in-law; residence orders.
