SPECIFIC RELIEF ACT, 1963 AND LIMITATION ACT, 1963 - KEY PROVISIONS
Source type: Starter notes (verify against the official Bare Act on indiacode.nic.in before relying on them)

### Specific Relief Act Section 5 | Recovery of specific immovable property (suit based on title)
A person entitled to possession of specific immovable property may recover it in the manner provided by the CPC.
This is the regular suit for possession based on ownership/title. Limitation: 12 years from when the defendant's possession becomes adverse to the plaintiff (Limitation Act, Article 65).

### Specific Relief Act Section 6 | Summary suit for possession after illegal dispossession
If a person is dispossessed of immovable property without his consent and otherwise than in due course of law, he (or anyone claiming through him) may sue to recover possession, regardless of any other title set up in the suit.
Conditions: the suit must be filed within 6 months from the date of dispossession; it cannot be filed against the Government; no appeal lies and no review is allowed from an order or decree under this section (Section 6(3)), but a separate suit to establish title is still possible (Section 6(4)).
Use case: tenant or occupant forcibly thrown out by landlord/owner without court order - quick remedy based only on prior possession, not title. Settled possession is protected even against the true owner (Rame Gowda v. M. Varadappa Naidu, (2004) 1 SCC 769).

### Specific Relief Act Section 7 and 8 | Recovery of specific movable property
A person entitled to possession of specific movable property may recover it as provided by the CPC (Section 7). A person in possession or control of an article who is not the owner may be compelled to deliver it to the person entitled (Section 8), e.g. where the article is held as agent or trustee, or compensation in money would not be adequate relief.

### Specific Relief Act Section 10 | Specific performance of contracts (after 2018 amendment)
After the Specific Relief (Amendment) Act, 2018 (in force from 1 October 2018), specific performance of a contract SHALL be enforced by the court, subject to Section 11(2), Section 14 and Section 16. Earlier it was discretionary.
Section 14: contracts that cannot be specifically enforced - e.g. where a party has obtained substitute performance, contracts involving continuous duty the court cannot supervise, contracts so dependent on personal qualifications that the court cannot enforce material terms, and contracts which are by nature determinable.
Section 16(c): the plaintiff must prove that he has performed or has always been ready and willing to perform the essential terms of the contract on his part (readiness and willingness).
Section 20 (substituted in 2018): substituted performance - the party who suffers breach may get the contract performed through a third party at the cost of the defaulting party, after giving 30 days' written notice.
Limitation for specific performance: 3 years from the date fixed for performance, or if no date is fixed, from when the plaintiff has notice that performance is refused (Limitation Act, Article 54).
Use case: seller took advance under an agreement to sell a house/plot and is now refusing to execute the sale deed. Buyer files a suit for specific performance; can also seek a temporary injunction against sale to third parties, and register a lis pendens (Transfer of Property Act Section 52 applies once suit is filed).
Note: an unregistered agreement to sell can still be received as evidence of the contract in a suit for specific performance (Registration Act, Section 49 proviso).

### Specific Relief Act Section 26 | Rectification of instruments ; Section 27 | Rescission of contracts
Section 26: where through fraud or mutual mistake a written contract or instrument does not express the real intention of the parties, either party may sue for rectification.
Section 27: a party may sue to have a contract rescinded where it is voidable or terminable by the plaintiff, or where it is unlawful for causes not apparent on its face and the defendant is more to blame.

### Specific Relief Act Section 31 | Cancellation of instruments
Any person against whom a written instrument is void or voidable, and who has reasonable apprehension that if left outstanding it may cause serious injury, may sue to have it declared void or voidable and cancelled. If the instrument was registered, the court sends a copy of its decree to the registration office to note the cancellation.
Limitation: 3 years from when the facts entitling the plaintiff to have the instrument cancelled first become known to him (Limitation Act, Article 59).
Use case: forged or fraudulent sale deed, gift deed obtained by undue influence, sale deed executed by someone without title.

### Specific Relief Act Section 34 | Declaratory decrees
Any person entitled to any legal character or right to property may sue for a declaration of that right against anyone denying it.
Proviso: no court shall make a mere declaration where the plaintiff, being able to seek further relief (e.g. possession or injunction), omits to do so.
Limitation: 3 years from when the right to sue first accrues (Limitation Act, Article 58).
Use case: when title is disputed or under a cloud, a suit for injunction alone is not enough; the plaintiff should sue for declaration of title along with possession/injunction (Anathula Sudhakar v. P. Buchi Reddy, (2008) 4 SCC 594).

### Specific Relief Act Sections 36 to 42 | Injunctions
Section 37: temporary injunctions are regulated by the CPC (Order 39); perpetual injunction is granted by decree at the end of the suit.
Section 38: perpetual injunction to prevent breach of an obligation, including invasion of the plaintiff's right to or enjoyment of property (e.g. trespass, interference with possession).
Section 39: mandatory injunction - court can compel performance of acts, e.g. demolition of an illegal construction or removal of encroachment.
Section 41: situations where an injunction cannot be granted - e.g. to restrain a person from instituting judicial proceedings, where an equally efficacious relief is available, where the plaintiff's conduct disentitles him, or where the plaintiff has no personal interest. After 2018, Section 20A: no injunction in a suit relating to an infrastructure project if it would impede or delay the project.
Use case: neighbour obstructing a common pathway, encroachment on land, blocking light and air, interfering with peaceful possession.

### Limitation Act Sections 3, 5, 14, 18, 27 | General rules
Section 3: every suit, appeal or application filed after the prescribed period shall be dismissed, even if limitation is not raised as a defence.
Section 5: delay in filing an appeal or application (other than an application under Order 21 CPC) may be condoned if sufficient cause is shown. Section 5 does NOT apply to suits.
Section 14: time spent bona fide prosecuting another civil proceeding with due diligence in a court without jurisdiction is excluded.
Section 18: a written, signed acknowledgment of liability made before the expiry of limitation starts a fresh period of limitation.
Section 19: a part-payment of debt or interest made before the expiry of limitation, and acknowledged in writing, also starts a fresh period.
Section 27: at the end of the limitation period for a suit for possession of property, the right to that property itself is extinguished (basis of adverse possession).

### Limitation Act Schedule - common limitation periods for civil suits and appeals
- Money lent (loan): 3 years from when the loan is made (Article 19).
- Compensation for breach of contract: 3 years from when the contract is broken (Article 55).
- Specific performance of contract: 3 years from date fixed for performance, or from notice of refusal (Article 54).
- Cancellation or setting aside of an instrument or decree, or rescission of contract: 3 years from knowledge of the facts (Article 59).
- Declaration (other than specifically provided): 3 years from when the right to sue first accrues (Article 58).
- Possession of immovable property based on previous possession (not title), after dispossession: 12 years from dispossession (Article 64).
- Possession of immovable property based on title: 12 years from when defendant's possession becomes adverse (Article 65).
- Landlord to recover possession from tenant: 12 years from when the tenancy is determined (Article 67).
- Person excluded from joint family property to enforce right to share: 12 years from when the exclusion becomes known (Article 110).
- Libel / slander (defamation): 1 year (Articles 75 and 76).
- Any other suit not specifically provided for (residuary): 3 years from when the right to sue accrues (Article 113).
- Appeal under CPC: 90 days to the High Court; 30 days to any other court (Article 116).
- Substitution of legal representatives of deceased party: 90 days from death (Article 120).
- Setting aside ex parte decree: 30 days (Article 123).
- Review of judgment (court other than Supreme Court): 30 days (Article 124).
- Execution of a decree (other than mandatory injunction): 12 years (Article 136).
- Any other application (residuary): 3 years from when the right to apply accrues (Article 137).
Special laws have their own periods - e.g. consumer complaint: 2 years from the cause of action (Consumer Protection Act, 2019, Section 69).

### Adverse possession
A person in open, continuous, hostile and uninterrupted possession of another's immovable property for 12 years (30 years against the Government - Article 112 for suits by the Government) may claim title by adverse possession; the true owner's right is extinguished under Section 27 of the Limitation Act.
A person who has perfected title by adverse possession can file a suit to protect his possession and declare title - adverse possession can be used as a sword, not only a shield (Ravinder Kaur Grewal v. Manjit Kaur, (2019) 8 SCC 729).
Permissive possession (tenant, licensee, family member with permission) is not adverse possession.
